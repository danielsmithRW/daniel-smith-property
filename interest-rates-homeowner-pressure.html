INTEREST RATES • DANIEL'S TAKE • 29 OCTOBER 2026

Another rate rise? What I'm hearing from homeowners right now.

There is plenty of discussion around another RBA rate rise in November, and already speculation about December. The economic numbers matter, but so do the conversations happening around kitchen tables.

Why is another rate rise being talked about?

Australia's latest inflation figures have put interest rates firmly back in the conversation. The Consumer Price Index rose 4.3% over the year to September, up from 4.0% in August. Trimmed mean inflation, which helps show the underlying trend, was 3.6%. Both remain above the Reserve Bank's 2 to 3% inflation target.

Cash rate: 4.60%, effective 30 September 2026

September annual CPI: 4.3%

September annual trimmed mean: 3.6%

Housing inflation: 5.2%

Transport inflation: 8.7%

Non-discretionary inflation: 5.2%, compared with 3.2% for discretionary items

The RBA's next decision is due on 3 November. Financial markets and economists are discussing the possibility of another increase, while December is also entering the conversation. But speculation is not a decision. Nobody knows the outcome until the RBA announces it.

Inflation doesn't always feel like excessive spending.

This is where I think there can be a disconnect between the economic conversation and what households actually experience. When the cost of electricity rises, you have not necessarily used more electricity. When petrol rises, many people still need to drive to work. When insurance rises, you have not suddenly bought more insurance. When groceries become more expensive, feeding the family has not become discretionary spending.

Higher spending in dollar terms does not always mean households are buying more. Sometimes the same life simply costs more.

The latest ABS figures are interesting in that context. Non-discretionary inflation was 5.2% over the year to September, compared with 3.2% for discretionary items. Housing was also the largest contributor to annual inflation, rising 5.2%.

What I'm hearing from homeowners.

Through real estate I speak with homeowners across the Shoalhaven every week. Increasingly, some of those conversations are not about chasing the next dream home or trying to time the top of the market. They are about understanding the household position.

Mortgage repayments have increased. Electricity, insurance, groceries, fuel and other everyday expenses have increased. For households that purchased when borrowing conditions were very different, or families that were already operating without much spare room in the budget, another increase matters.

I am hearing more questions like:

  • What is our property actually worth now?
  • How much equity do we have?
  • Can we keep absorbing higher repayments?
  • Do we refinance or use savings?
  • Would downsizing put us in a stronger position?
  • If we did sell, what would our options actually look like?

That does not mean every person asking those questions is in mortgage stress, and it certainly does not mean they are about to sell. Often they are simply trying to understand their options before they need to make a decision.

The national picture needs some context too.

It is important not to overstate what I am seeing locally. The RBA's September meeting minutes said household borrower balance sheets generally remained sound, arrears were low and most mortgage borrowers still held savings buffers. At the same time, scheduled mortgage payments had risen to around 10% of household disposable income, around their 2024 peak.

Both things can be true. The national mortgage system can remain broadly resilient while individual households feel considerably more pressure in their weekly and monthly budgets.

What could another increase mean for property?

For some homeowners, another repayment increase would be frustrating but manageable. For others, particularly those already reviewing every part of the household budget, it could be the point where they start looking more seriously at their options.

Buyers are affected too. Higher rates can change borrowing capacity, repayments and the price range people are comfortable pursuing. That does not automatically tell us what property prices will do. Local supply, buyer demand, employment, confidence and the number and quality of homes available all matter.

What it does tell me is that finance will continue to play a major role in the decisions buyers and homeowners make across the Shoalhaven.

What I'm seeing locally.

I don't think we should underestimate how much households adjust before making a major property decision. People cut spending, restructure debt, work more, use savings and change plans before selling a home they would otherwise prefer to keep.

But every household has a limit to what its budget can absorb.

That is why I think one of the most useful things a homeowner can have right now is information. Knowing what your property may be worth does not mean you have to sell it. Sometimes it simply gives you another piece of the puzzle when considering your mortgage, your equity and what you want the next few years to look like.

THE SIMPLE VERSION

You don't need to be selling to understand your position.

If interest rates and the cost of living have you reviewing the numbers, understanding where your property sits in the current local market can be useful. No assumption that you are selling, just a clearer picture of the property side of the equation.
